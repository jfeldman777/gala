Suppose the teachers agree to develop higher levels in the children, and everyone agrees that this isn't in the school curriculum or in the approved methodology.

"How can this be done?" the teachers ask.

I used to answer:
use the project method.

But what is the project method? Here's AI on that.

The project method appeared in the **USA at the start of the 20th century**.

- Its roots go back to the philosophy of **John Dewey (1859–1952)** — an American philosopher and educator, a proponent of "learning by doing."
- His student and colleague, the educator **William H. Kilpatrick (1871–1965)**, published a work in **1918** called _"The Project Method,"_ which was the first to describe this approach in detail and give it its name.

It is Kilpatrick who is usually called the author of the project method, with Dewey as its intellectual inspiration.

The method reached Russia in the 1920s and was actively introduced in schools (under the name "project method" or "problem-based learning method"), but in 1931 it was officially banned as "not corresponding to the tasks of the school." Its return came only in the 1990s, when the emphasis was again placed on students' independent work. (AI reference note)

However, there are nuances. 1) Dewey and Kilpatrick emphasized the difference between school tasks and real tasks. 2) Where do real tasks come from? The teacher organizes the process so that real tasks arise on their own, and the children only take on solving them and see them through to the end.

Here
- the teacher distinguishes the educational from the real
- the teacher organizes the environment
- the children take on the task and see the solution through to the end
That is, Dewey and Kilpatrick place the emphasis on the second level for the teacher, and the third for the student.

But can it be arranged so that the emphasis falls on the higher levels — 4, 5, 6 — for the student, say?

It can. Let's start with a simple example that shows how the field of attention changes.

## You Bought an Electric Kettle

You buy an electric kettle, bring it home and put the box on a clean table. For now, there is one object in your field of attention: the box. Level 1.

You open the box, take out the parts and papers, and spread them out on the table. Now there are several objects in front of you. Level 2.

The papers point to several things you might do. The receipt records the purchase, the warranty covers a possible breakdown, the repair shop addresses tell you where to go if it breaks, and the instructions in several languages explain how to use the kettle. Your field of attention holds several processes. Level 4.

Right now, you want to get the kettle running. You take a step back, pick up the short instructions in your own language and follow the pictures step by step. You put the other papers away for later. Your field of attention is back to one process. Level 3.

Now you have to work out how to connect the kettle to two networks: the water supply and the electricity supply. Where will you put it? How will you fill it? Which outlet will the cord reach? You have to improvise and decide on the spot. Level 5.

You look at the instructions again and notice a circuit diagram and an explanation of what happens inside the kettle. Now you understand how it works. Level 6.

You also find a list of things that could be risky. Don't turn it on without water or with the lid open. You look at the kettle in terms of what could go wrong. Level 7.

Finally, you're drinking tea and thinking you should invite some guests over. Level 8.

For a group project that engages levels 4, 5 and 6, see [2.3. A Hike in Nature](https://jfeldman777.github.io/gala/en.html?p=2.3).
That's no longer Dewey and no longer Kilpatrick. That's Feldman.
