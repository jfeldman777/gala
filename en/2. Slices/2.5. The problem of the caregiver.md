You're a caregiver, you have a group of children in your charge.
They keep starting up different activities.
Let's agree right away: you don't kill all their activities, and you don't force them to keep doing yours.
That you rely on their actions and their energy.
How do you act so that the system doesn't spin out of control (which is possible)?

The general rule is this.

If a child starts an even-numbered activity (we're playing, we find it interesting), then by definition it isn't destructive. Here you can help, or you can choose not to respond at all (if you don't have the resources for it).

See "The Experience of Difficult Victories."

But if a child starts an odd-numbered activity, you must respond.

1 — I want, 3 — I'm first, 5 — I'll take the risk, 7 — I have a brilliant idea.

You need to respond with plus one — the next even level.

I want — this is allowed, that is not (1–2)
I'm first — besides you, there are others too (3–4)
I'll take the risk — but have you thought it through? There are options with no risk at all (5–6)
I have a brilliant idea — but here are its long-term consequences (7–8)

The Experience of Difficult Victories
What does a child remember? Which part of their experience disappears — and which becomes the ground for
new successes?

There is a very simple rule
Your Failure is a bad support. Your success is a good one.
But notice the word YOUR — it must be YOUR effort that leads to victory — not the will of the gods and not a gift of fate — that is not yours and does not count for you.

But a child is a child and not an adult precisely because they cannot (given the balance of forces and needs) achieve success alone, without adults. And growing up is exactly the turning of a child who is helped into a youth who manages, and then into an adult — who helps others.

That is: one must help — and success matters — but one's own effort matters still more, for that alone makes experience solid.

So an adult must dose help exactly — and then the child develops in the best possible way.

**The grandmother effect.**
A small child's development depends directly on how precisely the mother doses her help in order to create for the child the **Experience of Difficult Victories**. But such dosing is hard work; it needs constant attention and strength. So the mother needs to rest. The grandmother gives her that chance. And the grandmother is not obliged to develop the child in that time: it is enough to keep comfort and safety for them until the mother has rested and can again dose her help precisely.
