Once Yakov Feldman and Vladimir Yezersky were discussing how to formulate a unique selling proposition for the product Discourse of Feldman. And they formulated one. But after the discussion YF decided to trace how the context had quietly shifted in the course of the talk. Here is what he found.

An ordinary person: I simply drift with the current.

Feldman's person: sometimes I simply drift with the current. Sometimes I choose which current to drift with.

Do I choose fortunately? Successfully? NOT ALWAYS. I would like to do better.

The notions Fortunate and Successful (and their pairs Unfortunate and Unsuccessful) say that in the second case we have a system with a potential for feedback — it is able to learn from mistakes. In the first case — a system without that potential.

Let us call a system with that potential an FS-system.

FS-systems must somehow measure their success. There are two types of such measurement.

The first type measures the success of the whole life up to and including the present moment.

The second type measures the success of one fragment of life — this fragment can be marked as a situation or as a task. The success of the whole life can then be estimated as the sum of the successes of all the fragments into which it falls apart.

YF asked an AI to assess how original the approach is. Here is the AI's answer.

These assumptions join several different traditions. None of the authors I know, as far as I can see, reproduces the whole construction, but there are fairly close correspondences.

The closest authors:

- **Daniel Kahneman** — distinguishes the _experiencing self_ and the _remembering self_. The first evaluates separate moments, the second — a life or an episode of it as a whole. This is an almost direct analogue of the two ways of measuring: current fragments and the whole life lived. Kahneman's important correction: the overall evaluation of a life is not equal to a simple sum of the evaluations of moments.

- **Ed Diener** — studies _subjective well-being_: life satisfaction as a global evaluation, and positive/negative experiences as local indicators. This is an empirically developed version of your distinction between two types of measurement.

- **Aristotle** — treats eudaimonia as the success, or "fulfilledness," of a human life as a whole. It cannot be judged from a single episode: a completed structure of the life is required. He is especially important as an opponent of the thesis that the success of a life is the sum of the successes of its fragments.

- **Jeremy Bentham** and, in part, **John Stuart Mill** — give the model closest to adding up fragments: the well-being of a life can be thought of as an aggregate of pleasures and pains. In Mill the sum is already non-uniform, because pleasures differ in quality.

- **John Dewey** — understands human action as a cycle: situation → action → consequences → revision of habits → new action. A person learns from the resistance of the environment and from unsuccessful consequences. This is a good philosophical version of an FS-system.

- **Chris Argyris and Donald Schön** — distinguish single-loop and double-loop learning. In the first case a person changes the action in order to reach the former goal; in the second, he revises the goals themselves, the criteria of success, and the initial premises. For Feldman this is especially important: a person can not only move better along the chosen current, but also change the choice of current.

- **Norbert Wiener** and **W. Ross Ashby** — create a common language of feedback, error, regulation, and adaptation. They describe, however, first of all the formal structure of systems; carrying that structure over onto a human life requires an additional anthropology.

- **Herbert Simon** — treats the human being as a boundedly rational adaptive system. A person does not maximize success, but looks for a solution that is good enough, receives feedback, and corrects the next choice. His notion of _satisficing_ corresponds well to your "fortunate and successful — not always."

- **Jean-Paul Sartre** — poses radically the question of choosing a life project. A person does not only act inside given circumstances, but chooses what meaning and direction to give them. This corresponds to the passage from "I drift with the current" to "I choose the current," although Sartre has almost no formal model of feedback.

- **Alasdair MacIntyre** and **Paul Ricœur** — treat life as a narrative unity. A single situation acquires meaning only inside the story of a life, and overall success in life cannot be computed by simply adding up local victories. They are useful for criticizing too mechanical a fragmentation of life into tasks.

Three main families result:

| Feldman's assumption | Closest authors |
| --- | --- |
| A person chooses the direction of his own life | Sartre, Frankl, MacIntyre |
| A person learns through mistakes and feedback | Dewey, Popper, Argyris and Schön, Simon |
| Local success in life and success of the whole life can be distinguished | Kahneman, Diener, Aristotle |
| A whole life can be evaluated as the sum of fragments | Bentham; in part, contemporary economics of well-being |
| A whole life does not reduce to the sum of fragments | Aristotle, Kahneman, Ricœur, MacIntyre |

If one keeps only the **most precise points of contact**, I would keep five: **Kahneman, Dewey, Argyris and Schön, Simon, Aristotle**.

The originality of Feldman's construction may consist precisely in joining three things that are usually separate: **the choice of a life-current, learning by feedback, and two scales for evaluating success**.

SECOND STAGE

A locally optimal strategy

Before the action itself, stop for a moment and think. Gather what we know about this fragment.

Gather what is known about oneself.

By comparing these two kinds of knowledge, compute the probability of success.

If the probability is high, accept the fragment.

If the probability is low, avoid the fragment.

An additional option — rebuild oneself so that the new pair of knowledges yields success.

Here the model becomes considerably more definite. This is no longer simply learning from experience, but a **local strategy for choosing an action on the basis of the fit between a person and a situation**.

The nearest authors and concepts:

| Element of the strategy | Authors and concepts |
| --- | --- |
| Stop before acting and assess the situation | John Dewey — reflective thinking; Daniel Kahneman — the shift from automatic System 1 to analytic System 2 |
| Gather knowledge about the situation | John Dewey; Herbert Simon — bounded rationality |
| Gather knowledge about oneself | Albert Bandura — self-efficacy; Donald Super — the self-concept |
| Estimate the probability of success | Thomas Bayes; Leonard Savage — subjective probability and decision theory |
| Accept the fragment when the probability is high | Herbert Simon — choosing a good-enough option (_satisficing_) |
| Avoid it when the probability is low | Richard Lazarus and Susan Folkman — threat appraisal and avoidant coping |
| Change oneself in order to fit the situation | Jean Piaget — accommodation; Bandura — development of competence; Chris Argyris and Donald Schön — learning |
| Compare the person and the environment | Kurt Lewin — behavior as a function of person and environment; theories of _person–environment fit_ |

Especially close models:

1. **Kurt Lewin**

Lewin formulated behavior as a function of the person and the environment:

\[ B=f(P,E) \]

Feldman has a more concrete version:

\[ P(\text{success})=f(\text{knowledge of oneself},\text{knowledge of the fragment}) \]

A situation is not successful or unsuccessful in itself. Success arises from the fit of a particular person to a particular situation.

2. **Albert Bandura**

His notion of **self-efficacy** means a person's estimate of his own ability to cope with a particular task. The person takes into account:

- the demands of the situation;
- his own abilities;
- previous experience;
- the expected consequences.

But Bandura distinguishes two judgments:

\[ P(\text{I will be able to perform the action}) \]

and

\[ P(\text{the action will lead to the result}) \]

This is an important refinement for Feldman's model: a low probability of success may be explained either by a lack of abilities or by the structure of the situation itself.

3. **Herbert Simon**

Simon is closest to the notion of a **locally optimal strategy**. Because a person has incomplete knowledge and limited time, he cannot compute the absolutely best choice in life. He chooses the option that seems successful enough given the information available.

This is not a global optimization of life, but a sequence of local decisions:

\[ \text{search} \rightarrow \text{evaluate} \rightarrow \text{accept what is acceptable, or keep searching} \]

A problem arises here, however: a series of locally reasonable decisions can lead to a globally unsuccessful life.

4. **Richard Lazarus and Susan Folkman**

In their theory a person first makes two appraisals:

- **primary appraisal:** what the situation is — an opportunity, harm, or a threat;
- **secondary appraisal:** do I have the resources to cope with it.

This corresponds almost literally to Feldman's two sets of knowledge: knowledge of the fragment and knowledge of oneself.

After the comparison a person can:

- enter the situation;
- avoid it;
- change the situation;
- change his own attitude or abilities.

5. **Jean Piaget**

The additional option — "rebuild oneself" — corresponds to **accommodation**. Piaget distinguishes:

- **assimilation** — taking the situation into the person's already existing structure;
- **accommodation** — changing one's own structure to meet the demands of the situation.

In Feldman's terms:

\[ \text{the situation fits me} \]

or

\[ \text{I change in order to fit the situation} \]

But the model lacks the symmetrical fourth option: **change not oneself, but the fragment or the environment**.

So a complete scheme could look like this:

1. Accept the fragment as it is.
2. Avoid it.
3. Rebuild oneself to fit the fragment.
4. Rebuild the fragment to fit oneself.

The last two options form a pair:

\[ \text{adaptation of the person to the environment} \quad\leftrightarrow\quad \text{transformation of the environment by the person} \]

The main theoretical danger of the second stage is mixing up **the probability of success** and **the value of the result**. A high probability does not yet make a fragment worth accepting. A locally rational decision requires at least two quantities:

\[ EU(a)=P(\text{success}\mid a)\times V(\text{success}) - P(\text{failure}\mid a)\times C(\text{failure}) \]

That is, one must ask not only "will it work?" but also:

- Is the success worth it?
- What is the cost of the attempt?
- What will be missed?
- Is the success compatible with the overall strategy of life?

Thus the most precise authorial correspondences here are: **Lewin — the fit of person and situation; Bandura — the estimate of one's own possibilities; Simon — a local acceptable choice; Lazarus — the double appraisal of situation and resources; Piaget — rebuilding oneself**.

THIRD CLARIFICATION

At the point of choice a person has three frames.

1. Several options
2. One option, but one can avoid it
3. One option, avoidance is impossible, but one can choose a strategy of minimal damage

I asked an AI to assess the originality. If by **“the last paragraph”** one means “THIRD CLARIFICATION”:

> At the point of choice a person has three frames:
>
> 1. Several options.
> 2. One option, but one can avoid it.
> 3. One option, avoidance is impossible, but one can choose a strategy of minimal damage.

I would rate the originality as **fairly high precisely at the level of classification**, but not of the separate elements.

### What already exists

In decision theory, case **1** is the standard problem of choice from a set of alternatives.

Case **2** is also well known: accept/refuse, approach/avoidance, and Hirschman is especially close with his _exit_: if the situation does not suit you, you can leave. ([Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/european-journal-of-political-research/article/exit-voice-and-loyalty-analytic-and-empirical-developments/E055A1628A612031EB02FE73459B2904))

Case **3** is not new in itself either. In _exit/voice_ research the situation in which **exit is impossible** is considered directly: a person is forced to stay and chooses a way of responding inside the given situation. There are even models of the case in which neither an effective exit nor an effective change of the situation is possible. ([Tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10967494.2021.1878314))

### Where I see originality

I have not yet found a known general classification that sets precisely **these three cases in one row as three types of a point of choice**:

**A. choice of the object of action**

\[ A_1,A_2,\ldots,A_n \]

**B. choice of participation**

\[ A \;/\; \neg A \]

**C. the choice of participation is already gone — what remains is the choice of how to pass through**

\[ A\ \text{is inevitable},\qquad S_1,S_2,\ldots,S_n \]

An interesting thing appears here: **choice does not disappear; it moves to another level**.

This, in my view, is stronger than the original formulation of "minimal damage." For example, a person falls ill — he no longer chooses the illness, but he chooses a strategy of conduct. An earthquake begins — the event is not chosen, the actions are. A person is drafted into the army — suppose there is no way out, but a space of strategies remains.

So a general principle can be stated:

> **When the space of choice of situations narrows, choice can remain as the choice of a strategy inside an inevitable situation.**

This formulation looks substantially more original.

**My preliminary estimate:** the three cases taken separately — low originality; **joining them into a classification of frames of choice — medium/high; the principle "choice of situation → choice of participation → choice of strategy inside the inevitable" — potentially high. Confidence ~75%.**

For a more serious check of originality one must look not for separate similar authors, but for **whether precisely this three-part typology of the space of choice exists anywhere**. A quick search did not show it to me; but that is not enough to claim priority.

So at the first stage we are ready to think, at the second — to measure, at the third — to format the point of choice.

It is precisely the combination of these three distinctions that makes our selling proposition unique.
