This is a true story that happened to us — to me and my wife. We were about to move to another flat. Of course that means packing things to take them along. But I noticed how differently we pack our things. I am a mathematician. I think in schemes. I split all things into a finite number of classes; each class holds its instances. I find an instance — I put it in a definite class. That is how scientific thinking works; it especially closely mirrors the course of mathematical thinking.

Artists think quite differently. They think in pictures — every detail matters. You must forget nothing, miss nothing. Moving each separate object turns into watching a film that has past episodes and future episodes. And instead of deciding about a particular pencil, the artist sees a film in which the biography of that pencil — past and future — makes a separate feature film. Now imagine you have a couple of thousand such pencils, and they are not simply pencils but things needed in the household, and imagine the volume of feature films an artist must watch when preparing to move to another flat. We rarely take such things into account — better say never — and we do not understand how different we all are.

There is a nuance, though. When my wife helped pack her friend's things, she did not watch films. Because those were someone else's things, she did not know their biographies and was not planning their future, so it was easy for her to sort things simply by sense.

The Cassandra effect is based on exactly that kind of thinking. Need I say that my wife forecasts the future far better than I can with all my logic — she simply sees that future as in a film.

As is known, the third element in the row with scheme and picture is text. It seems that processing text is qualitatively different from processing schemes and pictures. Perhaps. But apparently sign activity arose earlier than language and arose already in the animal world. So two very ancient abilities now coexist with a merely ancient one. People with a strong textual component write instructions easily and quickly, and read them easily and quickly. But when it comes to searching with the eyes and tightening with the hands, people who rely mainly on text find themselves at a disadvantage.
