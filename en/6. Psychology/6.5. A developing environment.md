Suppose teachers have decided to create, in a given subject, a developing environment — one in which a student can move through that environment at maximum speed, without a teacher who is always standing nearby.

What should they do?

### Codings

Create or select learning tasks so as to load all the Codings — but — NOTE — as **duplicates of one another, not as supplements**.

The same task should, wherever possible, be presented in different Codings. A student must not lose only because the material was presented in a Coding inconvenient for them.

### Warm-up

Drop Warm-up requirements wherever possible.

If they cannot be dropped, that is already a real limit of this activity: **not everyone is given this profession**.

### Levels

All Levels should be present, starting from the first.

In each topic there should be tasks of different Levels.

That does not mean the environment should prescribe a person's speed of movement.

It lets a person move slowly — distinguishing, looking at details, passing through the lower Levels — or quickly, **swallowing** what they already know.

At best the environment may recommend a route. **It must not forbid a transition.**

A person truly experienced in the given field runs at once to the binding — to Level 5.

An inexperienced person has to live through 1–2–3–4: open the box, see the parts, open the instructions, read the various notes — and only then switch it on.

But experience in a new field is not obvious.

If the field is already familiar, the lower Levels may be folded. If the field is new, they have to be gone through. But the person decides that.

### Context

For each class of tasks one must select **knowledge and Experience as the minimal base for that class of tasks**. (Necessary and sufficient.)

### Checking

To check mastery of a given Level, one may give the student a random task of that Level.

Better — not one, but **two or three tasks of that Level, chosen at random and independently**.

If the student solves them steadily, that Level in that topic is taken as mastered.

But the result of the check is a recommendation, not permission to move on. The environment must not forbid a person to try any Level at any moment.

### Impulse

A child **invariably starts activities of different Levels even in the course of one day**.

Age determines not the only Level available to them, but only **the maximum of this “Gaussian hat”**.

So one must not hold a child at some “age Level”.

The environment must be ready to catch activity of any Level that arises.

**Not one impulse should be lost. Each should, as far as possible, turn into the Experience of hard victories.**

The sequence of Levels describes the structure of the space of tasks, but does not prescribe the sequence of a person's movement through that space.

### A developing environment and the adult

What is the difference? An adult already has some algorithms and uses them; there is less chaos in their actions.
What can we do specifically for an adult?
In many cases an adult has formed negative stereotypes that get in the way of moving.
“Don't go here.”
“Don't do that.”
“Don't ask about that.”
And why, exactly? We are in a safe learning space. Here one may try everything.
It is another matter that in the real world we must keep safety rules — but first of all we must understand those rules. And fear (of breaking them) should come last.

### Failure

If a person has not solved a task, the environment does not decide for them what to do next. It offers a choice:

- **try again;**
- **here is a similar task;**
- **here is an easier task;**
- **here is a textbook.**

But the next move is chosen by **the person themselves**.

So failure does not mean an automatic drop in Level and does not mean a mandatory turn to explanation. The environment offers ways to continue, but **does not take away the person's control of their own trajectory**.

### Entering the environment

Ideally, on entry one should find out:

1. **What the person already knows:** what they know well, what a little, and what they do not know at all.
2. **Why they came:** what is their personal interest in the subject? Do they want to learn as much as possible, or did they come with a definite task or situation?
3. **How it is more convenient for them to begin moving:** what are their **Codings, Warm-up, Levels and Context**.

This is not needed in order to assign the person a route. On the contrary — to give them convenient entry points and the chance to **move from what they love and are used to toward the rest**.

That last sentence matters: diagnosis here is not a means of managing the person, but a way to **tune the environment for the start**, keeping freedom of further movement.
