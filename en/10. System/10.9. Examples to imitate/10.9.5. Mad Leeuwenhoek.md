## Leeuwenhoek: the man who saw an inhabited drop

Antonie van Leeuwenhoek was born on 24 October 1632 in Delft. He had no university education. He did not know Latin. He worked as a cloth merchant, then held several small municipal posts: he served at the town hall, was a surveyor, and inspected wine measures.

He made the microscopes himself.

His instrument little resembled a modern microscope. Between two metal plates sat a single tiny lens. The specimen was fixed on a needle in front of the lens. With screws it could be moved and brought into focus.

The instrument had to be held almost against the eye. The field of view was tiny. But a good single lens gave a clearer image than many compound microscopes of that time.

### The first letter

On 28 April 1673 Leeuwenhoek wrote his first letter to Henry Oldenburg, secretary of the Royal Society of London.

Leeuwenhoek himself did not belong to the learned world. He was introduced to the Society by the Delft physician and anatomist Reinier de Graaf.

In the letter Leeuwenhoek described observations of mould, a bee’s sting, a louse, and other small objects. He repeated some of Robert Hooke’s observations published in _Micrographia_, and added his own.

The letter was written in Dutch. In London it was translated, read at a meeting, and partly printed in _Philosophical Transactions_.

Oldenburg asked him to continue.

Thus began a correspondence that lasted nearly fifty years. The Royal Society holds about 190 of Leeuwenhoek’s letters. In the complete collection there are 364 letters connected with his observations and correspondence.

### Lake water

On 7 September 1674 Leeuwenhoek wrote to Oldenburg about water from Berkelse Mere near Rotterdam.

Green particles floated in the water. Among them Leeuwenhoek saw living creatures so small that the microscopic animals he had known before seemed huge beside them.

They moved in different directions, turned, and stopped. Leeuwenhoek called them _dierkens_ — little animals. In the English translation the word _animalcules_ appeared.

Today, among the creatures he described, one recognizes unicellular algae and protozoa.

This did not yet cause a great scandal. Microscopic mites and other small animals were already known. One could allow that animals of still smaller size might exist.

The next letter crossed the boundary of plausibility.

### Pepper water

In the spring of 1676 Leeuwenhoek put pepper in water. He wanted to understand why pepper burns the tongue.

On 24 April he began one experiment; on 26 April, another. Then he set up new experiments with rainwater, well water, and snow water. He looked at samples at once, after a few days, and after several weeks.

Leeuwenhoek expected to find a substance that produced the sharp taste. Instead, living creatures appeared in the steeped water.

On 9 October 1676 he sent Oldenburg a long letter — more than seventeen manuscript pages. In it he described dozens of successive observations.

Some creatures were relatively large and moved quickly. Others looked like tiny dots or rods. Leeuwenhoek tried to determine their size by comparing them with a grain of sand, a hair, and the eye of a louse. Then he estimated how many creatures fit in a drop.

He arrived at an incredible number: in a single drop there could be hundreds of thousands and even millions of living creatures.

In this letter is the first surviving description of bacteria.

### Why they did not believe him

The London scholars did not accuse Leeuwenhoek of lying. But they could not verify the report.

Before them was a merchant from Delft who wrote in Dutch and did not say how his best lens was made. Other microscopes did not show such numbers of creatures.

Besides, the observation did not fit the known picture of nature. An animal must have organs, be born, and feed. But how can all that fit into a point barely visible through a lens?

Oldenburg asked for independent witnesses.

In 1677 Leeuwenhoek’s microscope was visited by the pastors Benedict Hahn, Heinrich Cordes, and Alexander Petri, a Delft notary, a lawyer, and other respected townsmen. They looked into the instrument in turn.

On 5 October 1677 Leeuwenhoek sent to London the attestations of eight people. One witness estimated the number of creatures in a drop at ten thousand, another at thirty thousand, a third at forty-five thousand.

The signatures proved that Leeuwenhoek was not alone in seeing movement. But a pastor and a notary were not specialists in microscopy. The Royal Society needed its own observation.

### Hooke tries to repeat the experiment

Robert Hooke prepared pepper water and tried to see the “little animals.”

At first he saw nothing.

At the meeting of 1 November 1677 by the old English calendar Hooke showed water from a pump — to no effect. A week later he tried again with an improved microscope. Again nothing.

He made thin glass tubes, filled them with pepper water, and changed the lighting.

At last on 15 November by the English calendar — 25 November by the calendar in force in Delft — Hooke saw moving organisms. Then he showed them to the members of the Royal Society.

Now the observation no longer belonged to one man and one instrument.

On 1 February 1678 by the English calendar Hooke wrote to Leeuwenhoek: his observation was confirmed; the president and the fellows of the Society were surprised and satisfied.

Later the microscopic animals were shown to King Charles II.

### Spermatozoa

In November 1677 Leeuwenhoek reported another observation — living cells in seminal fluid.

He stipulated in advance the origin of the sample: it had been obtained not by a “sinful method,” but in marital relations with his wife.

Leeuwenhoek saw a multitude of bodies with long tails that moved like eels. He did not yet understand the union of spermatozoon and egg and attributed too great a role to male seed in the development of the embryo.

But the subject of reproduction for the first time became observable at the cellular level.

The Royal Society resolved to print the letter only insofar “as decency permits.”

### Plaque from his own teeth

On 17 September 1683 Leeuwenhoek wrote to the Society’s new secretary, Francis Aston.

He scraped plaque from his teeth, mixed it with water, and placed it before the lens. In the sample an enormous number of creatures moved.

Then he took material from the mouths of two elderly men who almost never cleaned their teeth. The forms and movements of the organisms differed.

Leeuwenhoek did not claim that bacteria cause tooth disease. For such a conclusion the observation was not enough. He established something else: the human body is constantly inhabited by creatures of which a person has no suspicion.

### Recognition

On 29 January 1680 Leeuwenhoek was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society.

He did not come to London and attended none of the Society’s meetings. The diploma was sent to him in Delft. He displayed it in a room and regarded it as one of the chief events of his life.

Leeuwenhoek continued his observations into deep old age. He examined blood, muscle, yeast, plants, insects, fish, seeds, minerals, and his own secretions.

He died on 26 August 1723, at the age of ninety. He dictated his last observations of illness to a physician and asked that they be sent to the Royal Society.

The main result of his work is not a list of objects discovered.

Leeuwenhoek created a new kind of evidence:

1. prepare a sample;
2. record when and how it was prepared;
3. observe it repeatedly;
4. compare it with other samples;
5. estimate sizes and numbers;
6. invite witnesses;
7. obtain an independent repetition.

Life existed in a drop of water before Leeuwenhoek. In 1676 it first became an event in human history — an observation recorded, contested, and repeated.
