What Aristotle definitely did not do — he did not build experimental equipment with his own hands. Galileo did. In his house (now a museum) there are inclined benches with a groove along which balls can be rolled. And in those grooves slits are cut, into which little bells can be inserted — and moved when needed.

What follows from this?

If you send a ball down an inclined plane, it accelerates. But if you place a mirror-image plane beside it, the ball, climbing up, slows down.
That means… on an ideally horizontal surface the ball neither slows down nor speeds up, but keeps its state of rest or of uniform rectilinear motion.

According to Aristotle — if you do not push, it does not move.
According to Galileo — if you do not push, it keeps its motion.

And the bells?

What does “accelerates” mean, exactly?

The problem is that Galileo has no mechanical clock — clocks of reasonable size and reasonable accuracy have not been invented yet.

But a human being has an innate sense of rhythm.

And Galileo rolls the balls so that a bell rings on contact with the moving ball. And he moves the bells until the ringing becomes rhythmic — occurring at equal intervals of time.

At last the goal is reached — and what now?

Now Galileo measures the distances between the bells. And lo and behold! They relate as odd numbers = 1, 3, 5, 7….

The Pythagoreans’ favorite toy —

1
1+3 = 4
1+3+5 = 9
1+3+5+7 = 16

On the right — squares of whole numbers.
In total:
The path traveled by a body that starts from rest and moves down an inclined plane is proportional to the square of the time of motion.

And this without having a clock!
