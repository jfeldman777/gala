On 13 September 1768, near the French village of Lucé, a glowing stone fell from the sky. Times were old — the most educated person in the village was the priest. Not that old, though: the priest turned out to be an amateur scientist; he drew up a report, attached witness accounts, and sent it by courier post to Paris, to the Academy of Sciences.

The academicians thought it over — and entrusted the investigation of this strange case to three young Frenchmen, one of whom was named Antoine Lavoisier.

The conclusion stated: it was not a stone that fell; lightning struck a stone. It happens. That lightning hits tall objects — church crosses, for example — and does not hit roadside stones, the young men did not know. Truly, old times.

In 1794 the German Ernst Chladni published a book collecting similar cases from different countries and eras. The plot repeated: a fireball in the sky, the sound of an explosion, stones of strange shape and non-local chemical composition.
Readers laughed and forgot.

On 13 December 1795, near Wold Cottage in Yorkshire, the labourer John Shipley saw a stone strike the ground a few metres from him. He was splattered with mud. The stone weighed about 25 kilograms. It drove into the earth about half a metre. The landowner Edward Topham gathered witness statements, dug up the stone, and put it on display.

In 1802 the chemist Edward Howard and the mineralogist Jacques Louis de Bournon compared stones from different falls — from Siena, Yorkshire, Benares, and elsewhere. The chemical composition repeated — metallic iron with an admixture of nickel.

On 26 April 1803 explosions sounded over the French town of L’Aigle. Several thousand stones fell over an area of several square kilometres. The French Institute sent there the young physicist Jean-Baptiste Biot.

Biot did not merely collect stones. He went from village to village, questioned witnesses, and marked find-spots on a map. People in different places independently described one fireball and one sequence of explosions. The stones did not lie just anywhere, but inside a definite region stretched along the body’s path of motion. The result was an event with place, time, participants, and material traces.

After Biot’s report, European scientists accepted: stones — meteorites — really do fall from the sky.

And of the young Lavoisier’s report the French kept a joke: stones cannot fall from the sky, because there are no stones in the sky.

The legalisation strip — 34 years; for the nineteenth century, not bad at all.
