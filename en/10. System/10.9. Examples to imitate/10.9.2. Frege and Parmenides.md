Parmenides’ date of birth is usually taken from Plato’s dialogue *Parmenides* and put at 515 BCE. He lived a long life, so roughly 515–435 BCE.

Parmenides set out his picture of the world in a separate poem (which has come down to us in numerous quotations). Clearly that picture pleased those who quoted it. Yet philosophers mention him not for the picture of the world, but for the thesis  
“Being is; non-being is not.”
From which one can logically derive many claims that contradict experience. And since the thesis seems impeccable, the usual conclusion is that experience cannot be trusted.

Many people (I will name only two: Leucippus and Aristotle) tried to refute Parmenides’ thesis. I tried as well — and that is what I will tell now.

As is well known, the quantifiers (“Always” and “There exists”) were introduced into logic by the great Gottlob Frege. And if Frege had found himself in an argument against Parmenides, he would probably have reasoned as follows.

A judgment about the real world must always be accompanied by the quantifier ALWAYS, or by its negation SOMETIMES (that is, there are cases when).

If SOMETIMES is not stated explicitly, the inexperienced logician substitutes — by default — ALWAYS.

Thereby he stops time — and then it is easy to derive the immobility of the world, the absence of motions and changes, the absence of differences where they are obvious, and so on.

That is: it is not the senses that lie; it is logic that has stacked the initial premises.

I have checked — no one invented such an argument against Parmenides.
